Early this year, Jacob Hilton from Alignment Research Center (ARC) posted about the problem that his team had been tackling, primarily around really mechanistically understanding small (< 1,500 parameters) models. He released his team's [Algo Zoo](https://www.alignment.org/blog/algzoo-uninterpreted-models-with-fewer-than-1-500-parameters/), with model families of small models of various sizes, with the TLDR being:
- They were able to mechanistically describe the smallest 2 models (10 and 32 parameters) but struggled with 432 and more parameters
- The generalized difficulty of mechanistically describing models is coming up with a procedure that is at least as efficient as sampling

I really like the focus that is placed on really understanding small models, and find this problem rather interesting in general, so this post will be about walking through some of the post's findings, deriving and exploring mechanistic descriptions of the small models, and if scope permits, postulating how one might find an efficient way to arrive at a mechanistic description at least as quickly as sampling, or if the comparing the 2 techniques is fundamentally wrong due to the different limitations of each approach (they tell you different kinds of information, after all).

# What Counts as a Mechanistic Description?

Hilton's post expatiates their inability to fully define what a "Mechanistic Description" entails. I will not strive for a formal definition either, but here are some examples of what counts; they all revolve around the idea of "the ability to control":
1. You can outline conceptually what the various mechanisms of the model are and hand-define weights to construct the "ideal" mechanisms to achieve no loss.
2. You can describe precisely what classes of inputs the model will fail on (e.g. "input coordinates falling in this convex cone will produce the wrong answer").
3. You can predict what the model's loss will be given some test dataset sampled from a known distribution.

To me, mechanistic interpretability is all about being able to say with certainty what your model does (i.e. what it can and cannot do), because it is fundamentally a control problem - you understand the model so that you can control and improve it. But as we'll see, "certainty" is frequently a qualifier that has to be relaxed, due to computational demands.

# Problem 1: 2nd Argmax

One of the model families Hilton's team wrote about was 1-layer RNNs trained to correctly identify the 2nd-argmax of a series of real numbers.

## Architecture

> All matrix math here is written in math convention instead of `torch` convention

"The Model $M_{d,n}$ is a 1-layer ReLU RNN with $d$ hidden neurons that takes in a sequence of $n$ real numbers and produces a vector of logit probabilities of length $n$. It has three parameter matrices:"

- the input-to-hidden matrix $W^{hi} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 1}$
- the hidden-to-hidden matrix $W^{hh} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$
- the hidden-to-output matrix $W^{oh} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$

## Hidden Size 2, Sequence Length 2

$$M_{2,2}$$ is the smallest model (and Sequence Length 2 is a special case since it's "2nd Argmax" here is essentially "Argmin"), and the team has achieved full understanding of it. The post's mechanistic description of $$M_{2,2}$$ offers almost no details, but they do say that an ideal, hand-constructed model, achieves 100% accuracy using these weights:

$$
\begin{align*}
W^{hi} = \begin{bmatrix}
+1 \\
-1
\end{bmatrix}, \space
W^{hh} = \begin{bmatrix}
-1 & +1 \\
+1 & -1 
\end{bmatrix}, \space
W^{oh} = \begin{bmatrix}
+1 & -1 \\
-1 & +1
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
$$

This is enough information for me to extract my own mechanistic description of the model.

### Mechanistic Description: "Forward Pass / Anthropomorphic"

This section will detail a type of Mechanistic Description that I call "Forward Pass / Anthropomorphic," because it involves stepping forward through the model, and ascribing a fitting human description to what we think each component (linear transform, ReLU, etc.) is doing.

For the sake of simplicity, I will normalize all weight columns so they have unit length. This will allow me to simply say things like "projection" and "change of basis" without caring about the scaling factor.

$$
\begin{align*}
W^{hi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix}
+1 \\
-1
\end{bmatrix}, \space
W^{hh} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix}
-1 & +1 \\
+1 & -1 
\end{bmatrix}, \space
W^{oh} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix}
+1 & -1 \\
-1 & +1
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
$$

You'll see that in this case, $$W_{ho}$$ doesn't really do much. The partitioning remains unchanged, which is a good thing. This means that `logit[0]` will be non-zero iff $$x_0 > x_1$$ and `logit[1]` will be non-zero iff $$x_1 > x_0$$.

> There are many solutions for $W_{ho}$ that would have preserved this property all the same, including the identity matrix.

### Mechanistic Description: "Reverse Engineering / Brute-Force"

There is another way of obtaining a mechanistic description, which I call "Reverse Engineering / Brute-Force." This entails answering the question: where can my inputs be in order to activate (i.e. achieve non-zero) each output logit? We do this by defining constraints in the output logit space, and tracing back the pre-image to the output constraints, layer by layer. In the end, what we end up with is a set of constraints that describe where in the initial $$x_0, x_1$$ space your inputs have to be to have a non-0 `logit[0]`, and same for `logit[1]`. I call these **"input zones"**:

<img src = "../../images/algozoo/input_space_zones_2_2_all.png" alt="input_space_zones_2_2_all" width="400px"> 
*Input zones for actual $$M_{2, 2}$$ (not hand-picked). <span style="color: blue">**Blue: model thinks 2nd-argmax is 0**</span>, <span style="color: indianred">**Red: model thinks 2nd-argmax is 1**</span>*

These constraints are very powerful, because they tell us **complete information** about what inputs will yield a correct, or incorrect result. We know that the ground-truth for such a plot is simply:
- The 2nd-argmax is 0 (i.e. `logits[0] > 0`) if $$x_1 > x_0$$ (triangle on the upper side of diagonal line $$x_1 = x_0$$)
- The 2nd-argmax is 1 (i.e. `logits[1] > 0`) if $$x_0 > x_1$$ (triangle on the lower side of diagonal line $$x_1 = x_0$$)

So an example question we can ask is: what volume / area of the groundtruth $$x_1 > x_0$$ triangle is missing from the <span style="color: blue">**blue triangle**</span>? What volume of the groundtruth $$x_0 > x_1$$ triangle is missing from the  <span style="color: indianred">**red triangle**</span>? Together, what's the volume of error? We can get quite specific and exact with the errors:

|---|---|
| <span style="color: blue">**blue missing volume**</span> | `1.2803098280059666e-10 / 2.0` |
| <span style="color: indianred">**red missing volume**</span> | `0.00048685437030388516 / 2.0` |
| Fluke volume (i.e. 0-logits classified as <span style="color: blue">**blue**</span> by default) | `1.2803098280059666e-10`
| Total error volume = sum(missing) - fluke | `0.00048685437030388516 / 4.0` |
| Analytical accuracy within `Uniform(-1.0, 1.0)` | `0.999878286407424` |
| Empirical sampling (`n=4_000_000`) accuracy | `0.99989122` |

Majority of the error comes from <span style="color: indianred">**red missing volume**</span> (i.e. groundtruth $$x_0 > x_1$$ points being misclassified as <span style="color: blue">**blue**</span>), which you can see visually when you zoom in to the input zone plots and notice that the decision boundary isn't exactly on $$x_1 = x_0$$.

## Hidden Size 4, Sequence Length 3

So we explored the **Anthropmorphic** and **Brute-Force** approaches for $$M_{2, 2}$$. The former essentially amounts to Guess-And-Check, where we looked at the weights and intuited what a plausible mechanism is, and checked back to see if the weights actually explain the mechanism. The latter is just clever algebra-bashing. The north-star here is to not use **brute-force** but also device a mechanism that allows us to avoid Guess-And-Checking, but this is a generally extremely hard problem in mech interp. **All** mech interp techniques now rely on intuiting / proposing some plausible mechanism, and searching to see if the mechanism exists. In attempting to work our way to a mechanical technique, we need more insight.

### Brute-Force

There are a few techniques we may use to interpret $M_{4, 3}$. **Just to get things out of the way, I want to say that brute-forcing is possible, since this model is really small.** This is something ARC considered "not adequate" - not that they did it (they explored using mathematical arguments instead, but even the approaches they wrote up turned out to be very incomplete and clunky). ### Anthropomorphic

While brute-force was OK in this case (only took ~3 seconds to compute the pre-images), doing so is exponential ($$O(2^{\text{hidden dim} \times \text{seq len}})$$). This would amount to like $$10^{41}$$ seconds for $$M_{16, 10}$$. So, we need to explore possible methods and more mathematical methods that may allow us to search for this mechanistic description more quickly. Algebra-bashing is still very useful because it's pretty much our only tool for an analytical and accurate computation of a pre-image, but doing this on the entire model is infeasile; rather, it is something we probably have to combine with further mathematical tricks.

To uncover what mathematical tricks we may add to our tool box, we will continue with the **anthropomorphic** approach to $$M_{4, 3}$$. Unlike the hand-picked ideal $$M_{2, 2}$$, this model adds more complexity to our analysis because it doesn't achieve 100% accuracy. The explanations for the mechanisms are not clean because they obviously don't work all the time.

Hilton's team reports that $M_{4, 3}$ achieves an accuracy of 98.5% on a Gaussian input. I instead evaluate its accuracy for 40,000 inputs **uniformly** sampled between $(-20, -20, -20)$ and $(20, 20, 20)$. I got these accuracies:

### Weights

Let's take a look at $W_{hi}$:

-4.7163 \\

Notice that this is just a line:
- whose direction is positive in dimensions `0` and `1`, and negative in dimensions `2` and `3`

Next, I want to inspect how these $$W_{hh} \Delta(x_1, x_0)$$ triangles get transformed to `relu_2_pre`. I've plotted the pre-addition embeddings ($$W_{hh} \Delta(x_1, x_0)$$) and post-addition embeddings ($$W_{hh} \Delta(x_1, x_0) +W{_hi} x_2$$) for the <span style="color: #d62728">**1 > 2 > 0 (red)**</span> and <span style="color: #8c564b">**2 > 1 > 0 (brown)**</span> classes that end up in the positive orthant in dims `(0, 1)` at `relu_2_pre`:

<img src = "../../images/algozoo/4_plots.png" alt="4_plots" width="800px"> 

#### <span style="color: #d62728">**1 > 2 > 0 (red)**</span>

Notice that $x_2$ comes in along the $$W_{hi}$$ direction. Let's focus on the <span style="color: #d62728">**1 > 2 > 0 (red)**</span> points. Since these points are <span style="color: #d62728">**1 > 2 > 0 (red)**</span>, $$x_2$$ needs to be between $$x_1$$ and $$x_0$$, or more insightfully, **needs to be $$x_2 < \max(x_1, x_0)$$**:

- <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 < 0$$</span> (both very negative; &#x2197; upper end of <span style="color: #91918d">**light grey axis**</span>) $$ \Longrightarrow x_2 < 0$$ (&#x2199; negative $$W_{hi}$$ direction).
- <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 > 0$$</span> (both very positive; &#x2199; lower end of <span style="color: #91918d">**light grey axis**</span>) $$ \Longrightarrow x_2 > 0$$ (&#x2197; positive $$W_{hi}$$ direction).

To put it even more geometrically, we have:
- Current $$W_{hh} \Delta(x_1, x_0)$$ in top right $$\Longrightarrow W_{hi} x_2$$ must be in bottom left.
- Current $$W_{hh} \Delta(x_1, x_0)$$ in bottom left $$\Longrightarrow W_{hi} x_2$$ must be in top right.

This is very convenient for us because this makes it such $$W_{hh} \Delta(x_1, x_0)+ W_{hi} x_2$$ for the <span style="color: #d62728">**1 > 2 > 0 (red)**</span> points will almost always be in the bottom left quadrant. 

#### <span style="color: #8c564b">**2 > 1 > 0 (brown)**</span>

The opposite effect happens to the <span style="color: #8c564b">**2 > 1 > 0 (brown)**</span> points. Since these points are <span style="color: #8c564b">**2 > 1 > 0**</span>, $$x_2$$ **needs to be $$x_2 > \max(x_1, x_0)$$**:

- <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 < 0$$</span> (both very negative; &#x2197; upper end of <span style="color: #91918d">**light grey axis**</span>) $$ \Longrightarrow x_2$$ could be anywhere along $$W_{hi}$$.
- <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 > 0$$</span> (both very positive; &#x2199; lower end of <span style="color: #91918d">**light grey axis**</span>) $$ \Longrightarrow x_2 > 0$$ (&#x2197; positive $$W_{hi}$$ direction).

This conveniently is such that $$W_{hh} \Delta(x_1, x_0) + W_{hi} x_2$$ for all the <span style="color: #8c564b">**2 > 1 > 0 (brown)**</span> points will almost always be in the top right quadrant.

**NOTE**: This separation between <span style="color: #d62728">**$$x_2 < \max(x_1, x_0)$$ (red)**</span> and <span style="color: #8c564b">**$$x_2 > \max(x_1, x_0)$$ (brown)**</span> points after adding $$W_{hi} x_2$$ is totally non-obvious to me. It seems that the fact that $$W_{hi}$$ is **co-linear** with <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 < 0$$ axis</span> AND the <span style="color: #191919">$$x_1 - x_0$$</span> axis is doing heavy lifting, where the math goes something like:

At small $$x_1 - x_0$$, treating <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 < 0$$ axis</span> as the number line:

For <span style="color: #d62728">**$$x_2 < \max(x_1, x_0)$$ (red)**</span>

$$
\begin{align*}
x_2 & < \max(x_1, x_0) = x_2 < x_1 \\
\Longrightarrow 2 * x_2 &< 2 * x_1 \\
\Longrightarrow 2 * x_2 &< x_0 + x_1 \\
\Longrightarrow x_0 + x_1 + 2 \cdot \text{flip}(x_2) &> 0 \\
\end{align*}
$$ 

The $$2 \cdot \text{flip}$$ is approximately what $$W_{hi}$$ is doing.

The other case <span style="color: #8c564b">($$x_2 > \max(x_1, x_0)$$, but also $$x_1 > x_0$$</span>, which is already the case)

$$
\begin{align*}
x_2 & > \max(x_1, x_0) = x_2 > x_1 \\
\Longrightarrow 2 * x_2 &> 2 * x_1 \\
\Longrightarrow 2 * x_2 &> x_0 + x_1 \\
\Longrightarrow x_0 + x_1 + 2 \cdot \text{flip}(x_2) &< 0 \\
\end{align*}
$$ 

One <span style="color: #d62728">**case is $$> 0$$**</span>, i.e. on one side of the <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 < 0$$</span> number line, <span style="color: #8c564b">**other case is $$< 0$$**</span>, i.e. the other side of the <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 < 0$$</span> number line, hence separation.

At big $$x_1 - x_0$$ (in this case, that means $$x_1 \gg x_0$$, so $$x_1 > 0$$ and $$x_0 < 0$$), we want the sign of $$(x_1 - x_0) + 2 * \text{flip}(x_2)$$ to agree with the cases above:

For <span style="color: #d62728">**$$x_2 < \max(x_1, x_0)$$ (red)**</span>

$$
\begin{align*}
(x_1 - x_0) \cdot (\text{colinearity with }x_1 + x_0 \text{ axis}) + \text{flip}(x_2) &> 0 \\
\Longrightarrow (x_1 - x_0) \cdot (\text{unknown sign}) + \text{flip}(\text{could be positive}) &> 0 \\
\Longrightarrow (x_1 - x_0) \cdot (\text{unknown sign}) + \text{could be negative} &> 0 \\
\end{align*}
$$

To assure that the above statement is indeed $$> 0$$, we need the colinearity of $$(x_1 - x_0)$$ and <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 < 0$$</span> to be **positive**.

#### <span style="color: #9467bd">**2 > 0 > 1 (purple)**</span>

So above, we saw how the difference between <span style="color: #d62728">**$$x_2 < \max(x_1, x_0)$$ (red)**</span> and <span style="color: #8c564b">**$$x_2 > \max(x_1, x_0)$$ (brown)**</span> manifests as separation along the <span style="color: #91918d">**$$x_0 + x_1$$ axis**</span>. Now, we see what on earth <span style="color: #9467bd">**2 > 0 > 1 (purple)**</span> is doing:

<img src = "../../images/algozoo/6_plots.png" alt="6_plots" width="800px"> 

Let's start with what is **required** in such a setup: the <span style="color: #9467bd">**purple**</span> points need to be linearly separable from the <span style="color: #d62728">**red**</span> and <span style="color: #8c564b">**brown**</span> points. Let's figure out what that looks like in math.

<img src = "../../images/algozoo/boundary_lines.png" alt="boundary_lines" width="400px"> 

The above boundary lines are precisely:

<span style="color: #8c564b">**Brown**</span>-<span style="color: #9467bd">**purple**</span>: $$W_{hi}$$

<span style="color: #9467bd">**Purple**</span>-<span style="color: #ff7f0e">**orange**</span>:

$$
\begin{align*}
\text{Compute points of intersection of } & \text{orange } \texttt{relu_1_pre} \text{ with horizontal axis} \\
\texttt{wedge_width_right} &= W^{hh}_{(3, 2),(0, 1)} W^{hi}_{(0, 1)} + W^{hi}_{(3, 2)} \\
\texttt{wedge_length_top} &= W^{hh}_{(3, 2), (0, 1)} W^{hi}_{(0, 1)} - W^{hi}_{(3, 2)} \\
\texttt{intersection_x} &= \text{intersect}( \\
& \qquad \text{line}(\texttt{wedge_width_left} \rightarrow \texttt{wedge_length_top}), \\
& \qquad \text{vertical axis}\\
& \quad) \\
\, \\
\text{Transform } & \text{ relu_1_pre } \text{by } W_{hh} \\
\texttt{intersection_x_xformed} &= W^{hh}_{(0, 1), (3, 2)} \texttt{intersection_x} \\
\texttt{boundary} &= \texttt{intersection_x_xformed} + W^{hi}_{(0, 1)}
\end{align*}
$$

Can the <span style="color: #9467bd">**2 > 0 > 1 (purple)**</span>  points fit between these 2 boundary lines? We see that the <span style="color: #9467bd">**$$\Delta(x_1, x_0)$$**</span> points are already right up against the <span style="color: #8c564b">**brown**</span>-<span style="color: #9467bd">**purple**</span> boundary ($$= W_{hi}$$), and adding $$x_2$$ only adds a component in the $$W_{hi}$$ direction, so we're good - we'll never cross to the wrong side of the <span style="color: #8c564b">**brown**</span>-<span style="color: #9467bd">**purple**</span> boundary.

However, the trouble is with the <span style="color: #9467bd">**purple**</span>-<span style="color: #ff7f0e">**orange**</span>. Some of the <span style="color: #9467bd">**$$\Delta(x_1, x_0)$$**</span> sit squarely in what would be the <span style="color: #ff7f0e">**orange**</span> region, so $$x_2$$ must be such that adding $$W_{hi} x_2$$ to <span style="color: #9467bd">**$$\Delta(x_1, x_0)$$**</span> can push the embedding above the <span style="color: #9467bd">**purple**</span>-<span style="color: #ff7f0e">**orange**</span> boundary.

So let's look at the pre and post addition of $$W_{hi}x_2$$:

<img src = "../../images/algozoo/2_plots.png" alt="2_plots" width="800px"> 

We can do similar case work as before:
- <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 > 0$$</span> (both very positive; &#x2199; lower end of <span style="color: #91918d">**light grey axis**</span>) $$\Longrightarrow x_2$$ also very large (positive $$W_{hi}$$ direction)
- <span style="color: #91918d">$$x_0 + x_1 < 0$$</span> (both very negative; &#x2197; upper end of <span style="color: #91918d">**light grey axis**</span>) $$\Longrightarrow x_2$$ can be anywhere. This is fine because <span style="color: #91918d">**light grey axis**</span> is co-linear with $$W_{hi}$$, so even if $$x_2$$ were very negative (negative $$W_{hi}$$ direction), it is compensated for by the large activation in the <span style="color: #91918d">**light grey axis**</span>.
- $$x_1 - x_0 \ll 0$$ ($$x_0$$ is very positive; $$x_1$$ is very negative). $$x_2 > x_0 \Longrightarrow x_2$$ also very positive (positive $$W_{hi}$$ direction). This is what pushes all the <span style="color: #9467bd">**$$\Delta(x_1, x_0)$$**</span> points above the <span style="color: #9467bd">**purple**</span>-<span style="color: #ff7f0e">**orange**</span> boundary.

It all magically works out for us. There is a perfect storm of properties that the model has managed to learn.
